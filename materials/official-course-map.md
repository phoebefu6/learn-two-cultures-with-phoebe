## Session 1 - Two ways to ask the data (Breiman 2001)

Breiman, L. (2001). Statistical Modeling: The Two Cultures (with comments and a rejoinder by
the author). Statistical Science 16(3), 199-231. doi 10.1214/ss/1009213726. **Read at source**
(33-page PDF including all four comments and the rejoinder).

Verified facts, all read at source (page numbers are the journal's):
- Abstract: "There are two cultures in the use of statistical modeling to reach conclusions from
  data. One assumes that the data are generated by a given stochastic data model. The other uses
  algorithmic models and treats the data mechanism as unknown." (p.199)
- The black box: x goes in, y comes out, "nature" inside. Two goals: **Prediction** and
  **Information** ("to extract some information about how nature is associating the response
  variables to the input variables"). (p.199)
- Data modeling culture: fills the box with e.g. linear regression, logistic regression, Cox
  model; "Model validation. Yes-no using goodness-of-fit tests and residual examination.
  Estimated culture population. 98% of all statisticians." (p.199)
- Algorithmic culture: box "complex and unknown"; decision trees, neural nets; "Model
  validation. Measured by predictive accuracy. Estimated culture population. 2% of
  statisticians, many in other fields." (p.199) These percentages are Breiman's ESTIMATE; Efron
  replied the prediction culture "at least around Stanford, is a lot bigger than 2%" (p.219).
- Road map names three lessons: Rashomon (multiplicity of good models), Occam (simplicity vs
  accuracy), Bellman (dimensionality, curse or blessing). (p.200)
- Career: seven years academic probabilist, resigned, thirteen years free-lance consulting,
  joined Berkeley Statistics in 1980. (p.200)
- Ozone project (mid-1970s, EPA): over 450 meteorological variables, seven years of data, first
  five years training, last two years test. (pp.200-201)
- Chlorine from mass spectra (EPA): 30,000 compounds, random 25,000 training / 5,000 test; LDA
  and QDA tried; a decision tree using 1,500 designed yes-no questions reached 95% accuracy on
  both chlorines and nonchlorines. (p.201)
- Perceptions on leaving consulting (p.201): (a) focus on finding a good solution; (b) "Live with
  the data before you plunge into modeling."; (c) search for a model that gives a good solution,
  either algorithmic or data; (d) "Predictive accuracy on test sets is the criterion for how good
  the model is."; (e) computers are an indispensable partner.
- A Berkeley friend's first question on hearing about decision trees: "What's the model for the
  data?" (p.202)
- Journals: virtually every article begins "Assume that the data are generated by the following
  model: ..." (p.202)
- "The conclusions are about the model's mechanism, and not about nature's mechanism." and "If the
  model is a poor emulation of nature, the conclusions may be wrong." (p.202)
- Faculty salary study: 25 other variables, gender coefficient significant at 5% "accepted as
  gospel"; Breiman: "Can the data gathered answer the question posed? Is inference justified when
  your sample is the entire population?" (pp.202-203)
- Goodness-of-fit: simulated 7-dimensional regression, tests did not reject linearity until the
  nonlinearity was extreme; Cleveland (Berkeley seminar, 1993) admitted residual analysis could
  not uncover lack of fit in more than four to five dimensions. (p.203)
- Predictive accuracy: put the same x down nature's box and the model's box; cross-validation
  (Stone 1974); Mosteller and Tukey (1977): "Cross-validation is a natural route to the indication
  of the quality of any data-derived quantity". (p.204)
- Table 2 (p.207): test-set error, forest vs single tree, e.g. Breast cancer 2.9 vs 5.9. NOTE:
  Breiman's "Cancer" is the 699-case, 9-variable Wisconsin (original) set, NOT the 569-case
  diagnostic set on our bench. Never conflate them.
- Occam dilemma: trees "A+ on interpretability", "a B on prediction"; forests "A+ predictors" and
  "on interpretability, they rate an F". (pp.206-208)
- "The goal is not interpretability, but accurate information." (p.210)
- Hepatitis (155 patients, 19 covariates): logistic regression error 17.4% (100 runs, 10% held
  out); random forest 12.3%; calling everyone a survivor gives a baseline 20.6% (32 deaths, 123
  survivors). Logistic regression "is not extracting much useful information from the data". Forest
  importance singles out variables 12 and 17, surrogates for each other. (pp.210-213)
- Variable importance by permuting one variable in the left-out cases and measuring the increase
  in misclassification (p.211). Our bench uses the same idea on the held-out rows.
- Conclusion: "I am not against data models per se." "The best solution could be an algorithmic
  model, or maybe a data model, or maybe a combination." (p.214)

Real-world stories for S1: ozone, chlorine, the Colorado court delay tree ("I knew those guys in
District N were dragging their feet", p.207), the faculty salary study, hepatitis.

## Session 2 - All models are wrong, some are useful (Box)

- Box, G. E. P. (1976). Science and Statistics. JASA 71(356), 791-799. **Read at source**
  (JSTOR scan). The famous sentence does NOT appear verbatim. It says, twice:
  - 2.3 Parsimony: "Since all models are wrong the scientist cannot obtain a 'correct' one by
    excessive elaboration." (p.792) ... "overelaboration and overparameterization is often the
    mark of mediocrity."
  - 2.4 Worrying Selectively: "Since all models are wrong the scientist must be alert to what is
    importantly wrong. It is inappropriate to be concerned about mice when there are tigers
    abroad." (p.792)
  - Science as "a motivated iteration" between theory and practice, drawn as a feedback loop;
    "the statistician knows ... that in nature there never was a normal distribution, there never
    was a straight line", yet useful results follow; "we cannot know that any statistical
    technique we develop is useful unless we use it."
  - Illustrated with R. A. Fisher at Rothamsted; coins "mathematistry" for theory detached from
    practice.
- Box, G. E. P. (1979). Robustness in the Strategy of Scientific Model Building. In R. L. Launer
  and G. N. Wilkinson (eds), Robustness in Statistics, Academic Press, 201-236. **Read at source**.
  Section heading, p.202: "ALL MODELS ARE WRONG BUT SOME ARE USEFUL". Then (p.203): the ideal gas
  law PV = RT "is not exactly true for any real gas, but it frequently provides a useful
  approximation and furthermore its structure is informative"; "For such a model there is no need
  to ask the question 'Is the model true?'. If 'truth' is to be the 'whole truth' the answer must
  be 'No'. The only question of interest is 'Is the model illuminating and useful?'." Model
  building "must be accomplished by iteration": tentative model, tentative analysis, criticism
  using residual diagnostic checks (Figure 1). Parsimony p.202: "when important aspects of the
  truth are simple, simplicity illuminates, and complication obscures". Also issued as MRC
  Technical Summary Report #1954, Univ. of Wisconsin, May 1979 (reported, the DTIC copy did not
  download).
- Box and Draper (1987), Empirical Model-Building and Response Surfaces: "Essentially, all models
  are wrong, but some are useful." **Reported** only; say so on the page.
- So the answer to "which paper": the IDEA is 1976 (twice), the exact SENTENCE is 1979, the
  "essentially" version is 1987.

What "useful" means operationally (the session's own synthesis, labelled as such on the page):
beats the base rate on rows it never saw (Breiman d); wrong only in ways that do not matter to the
decision (Box's tigers and mice); its structure tells you something (Box's "illuminating").

Cox's comment (read at source, p.217): "Descriptively appealing and transparent methods with a firm
model base are the ideal."; significance tests and intervals "are often lower bounds to real
uncertainty". Hoadley's Fat Scorecard (p.222): all models with 27 to 36 characteristics had the
same test performance; the 27-characteristic model had 162 score weights with p-values from 0.0001
to 0.984, only one below 0.05, "The confidence intervals for the 162 score weights were useless",
and it slightly outperformed a 2,000-weight neural network. Wrong by the textbook, useful by the test.

Bench canon used in S2 (see Canon): with all 30 features the logistic regression gets 170 of 171
held-out biopsies right, AND 7 of its 30 standardised coefficients point against their own
feature's correlation with "benign" on the training rows. They are NOT the size features: they
are mean compactness, mean symmetry, smoothness error, compactness error, concavity error,
fractal dimension error and worst compactness (build-along S2 output). Mechanism: 21 feature
pairs correlate above 0.9 in absolute value on the training rows, so weight is shared and traded
between near-duplicates; a feature can take a sign that corrects its neighbours rather than one
that describes itself. With the creators' 3 features: 4 numbers, 0 sign surprises, 167 of 171.

## Session 3 - Explore first, confirm once (Tukey)

(Title polished from "Look before you confirm": data-thinking s3 is "Look before you model".)

- Tukey, J. W. (1962). The Future of Data Analysis. Annals of Mathematical Statistics 33(1),
  1-67. doi 10.1214/aoms/1177704711. **Read at source** (full 68-page JSTOR scan, text layer).
  Printed page numbers:
  - p.2: "For a long time I have thought I was a statistician, interested in inferences from the
    particular to the general. But as I have watched mathematical statistics evolve, I have had
    cause to wonder and to doubt." ... "All in all, I have come to feel that my central interest is
    in data analysis, which I take to include, among other things: procedures for analyzing data,
    techniques for interpreting the results of such procedures, ways of planning the gathering of
    data to make its analysis easier, more precise or more accurate, and all the machinery and
    results of (mathematical) statistics which apply to analyzing data."
  - p.4: "we need to stress flexibility of attack, willingness to iterate, and willingness to study
    things as they are, rather than as they, hopefully, should be."
  - pp.5-6: three constituents of a science: "(a1) intellectual content, (a2) organization into an
    understandable form, (a3) reliance upon the test of experience as the ultimate standard of
    validity." "By these tests, mathematics is not a science"; data analysis is "a science, one
    defined by a ubiquitous problem rather than by a concrete subject."
  - p.6: (b1) "Data analysis must seek for scope and usefulness rather than security." (b2) "must be
    willing to err moderately often in order that inadequate evidence shall more often suggest the
    right answer." (b3) "must use mathematical argument and mathematical results as bases for
    judgment rather than as bases for proof or stamps of validity."
  - p.7: keep "using statistical techniques for the confirmatory appraisal of observations through
    such conclusion procedures as confidence statements and tests of significance." Quoting Martin
    Wilk: "The hallmark of good science is that it uses models and 'theory' but never believes them."
  - p.13: the danger of "the use of formal data-analytical procedures for sanctification, for the
    preservation of conclusions from all criticism, for the granting of an imprimatur."
  - pp.13-14, section 11 "Facing uncertainty": "Far better an approximate answer to the right
    question, which is often vague, than an exact answer to the wrong question, which can always be
    made precise."
  - p.64 closing: "the rocky road of real problems in preference to the smooth road of unreal
    assumptions, arbitrary criteria, and abstract results without real attachments. Who is for the
    challenge?"
  - The words "exploratory data analysis" as a named practice do NOT appear in 1962; the 1962
    paper uses "confirmatory appraisal" once. The explicit exploratory/confirmatory pairing is the
    1977 book and the 1980 paper (both reported).
  - ATTRIBUTION TRAP: Donoho 2017 quotes a list "Four major influences act on data analysis today"
    (formal theories; computers and display devices; larger bodies of data; quantification) right
    after his FoDA quotes. That list is NOT in the 1962 text read here (full-text search, 68 pages).
    Pages may quote it only as "a list Donoho attributes to Tukey", with the source unconfirmed.
  - Two versions of one aphorism: Tukey 1962 (read, above) and Cox 2001 "Better a rough answer to
    the right question than an exact answer to the wrong question, an aphorism, due perhaps to Lord
    Kelvin" (read). Teach both; the Kelvin origin is Cox's "perhaps", unconfirmed.
- Tukey (1977), Exploratory Data Analysis, Addison-Wesley: EDA as "detective work". **Reported**.
- Tukey (1980), We Need Both Exploratory and Confirmatory. The American Statistician 34(1), 23-25.
  **Reported** (JSTOR, not opened). The title itself is the claim the session teaches.
- Donoho 2017 (read at source): "Since John Tukey's coining of the term 'exploratory data
  analysis' (EDA), we all agree that every data scientist devotes serious time and effort to
  exploring data ... Such detective work adds crucial insights to every data-driven endeavor."
- Breiman 2001 perception (b), read at source: "Live with the data before you plunge into modeling."
- Why the confirmatory set must be untouched, read at source:
  - Hoadley (p.223), "The algorithm tuning dilemma": "in industry and academia, there is sometimes
    a little tinkering, which involves peeking at the test sample. The result is some bias in the
    test sample or cross-validation results." "How do you optimize your results and get an
    unbiased estimate of the generalization error?"
  - Hoadley (p.221-222): test performance "does not guarantee high performance on future samples,
    things do change"; Fair, Isaac redevelops core models about every 18 to 24 months.
  - Donoho: the CTF referee's test set "is sequestered" and scored automatically.
- Bench canon for S3: the peek (score on the training rows) gives the forest 398 of 398 (100.0%)
  against 167 of 171 held out (97.7%). The regression is 392 of 398 on training (98.5%) but 170 of
  171 held out (99.4%): a held-out set of 171 can flatter as well as punish; one biopsy is 0.6
  points.

## Session 5 - Fifty years of data science (Donoho)

Donoho, D. (2017). 50 Years of Data Science. Journal of Computational and Graphical Statistics
26(4), 745-766. doi 10.1080/10618600.2017.1384734. **Read at source** (published version; the
2015 Tukey Centennial preprint v1.00 also read). Facts:
- Opens from Tukey's 1962 paper (prepared for the John Tukey centennial).
- Chambers (1993) "Greater or Lesser Statistics, A Choice for Future Research"; Cleveland (2001)
  "Data Science: An Action Plan for Expanding the Technical Areas of the Field of Statistics" with
  six areas and allocations: multidisciplinary investigations 25%, models and methods for data
  20%, computing with data 15%, pedagogy 15%, tool evaluation 5%, theory 20% (quoted via Donoho).
- Breiman section: Donoho renames the cultures "generative" and "predictive" and says so in
  footnotes.
- Cox and Efron objected; Hoadley engaged enthusiastically (Donoho's reading).
- Common Task Framework (term from Mark Liberman): (a) a publicly available training dataset with
  features and a class label per observation; (b) enrolled competitors with the common task of
  inferring a prediction rule; (c) a scoring referee that runs submitted rules against a
  sequestered test set and reports the score automatically. Netflix Challenge ($1M; footnote: the
  winning algorithm was never implemented). Liberman's experience: error rates decline by a fixed
  percentage each year to an asymptote; progress from many small improvements ("a change of 1% can
  be a reason to break out the champagne"); shared data reused in unexpected ways.
- History via Liberman (reported by Donoho): J. R. Pierce's 1960s review sent US machine
  translation research "essentially to zero for decades"; IBM's Jelinek team and the Canadian
  Hansards; DARPA adopted CTF in the late 1980s with NIST as referee.
- "It is no exaggeration to say that the combination of a predictive modeling culture together
  with CTF is the 'secret sauce' of machine learning." CTF "is the single idea from machine
  learning and data science that is most lacking attention in today's statistical training."
- Six divisions of Greater Data Science (GDS): 1 Data Gathering, Preparation, and Exploration;
  2 Data Representation and Transformation; 3 Computing with Data; 4 Data Modeling; 5 Data
  Visualization and Presentation; 6 Science about Data Science. NUMBERING QUIRK, verified in the
  published PDF: the list puts Modeling 4th and Visualization 5th, but the section headings that
  follow read "GDS4: Data Visualization and Presentation" and "GDS5: Data Modeling". Pages cite the
  divisions by NAME and mention the quirk once.
- GDS1 "Some say that 80% of the effort ..." is Donoho's hedge, not a measurement: teach as "some
  say", never as a statistic.
- Data Modeling: "Each data scientist in practice uses tools and viewpoints from both of Leo
  Breiman's modeling cultures". Data modeling dominates academic teaching; GDS1 "is more important
  than" modeling "as measured using time spent by practitioners" (Donoho's claim, unmeasured here).
- Conclusion: "data science is the science of learning from data".
- Data science in 2065 (Donoho's forecast): "Many new common task frameworks will appear; however,
  the new ones would not always have prediction accuracy for their performance metric."

## Session 6 - Which culture is this question? (synthesis)

All four comments and the rejoinder, **read at source** (Statistical Science 16(3)):
- Cox (216-218): "based in part on a caricature"; "I would prefer to start with an issue, a
  question or a scientific hypothesis"; accepts that "there are situations where a directly
  empirical approach is better", naming short term economic forecasting and real-time flood
  forecasting; but "Often the prediction is under quite different conditions from the data" (the
  v-CJD epidemic in the UK; the effect on US cancer incidence of reducing medical X-rays by 10%);
  "Better a rough answer to the right question than an exact answer to the wrong question, an
  aphorism, due perhaps to Lord Kelvin"; "while I disagree with the main thrust ... I found it
  stimulating".
- Efron (218-219): "100 years of unbiasedness"; black boxes with "knobs to twiddle"; Rule 1 "New
  methods always look better than old ones."; Rule 2 "Complicated methods are harder to criticize
  than simple ones."; the prediction culture "is a lot bigger than 2%"; "The whole point of science
  is to open up black boxes"; hopes the paper is advocacy rather than "the confessions of a
  born-again black boxist".
- Hoadley (220-224): Fair, Isaac credit scoring since the 1960s as an algorithmic culture built by
  engineers; segmented scorecards; the Fat Scorecard; test sample overfitting and drift; the
  "Ping-Pong theorem" (each side could beat the other's revealed score); interpretable AND accurate
  is possible; lawyers and regulators need "super interpretability".
- Parzen (224-226): prediction vs information as "management" vs "science"; Student's t-test was a
  management tool at Guinness; "statistics has many cultures".
- Rejoinder (226-231): Cox is "in downright disagreement"; "Given a statistical problem, find a good
  solution"; "I would include Bayesians in the data modeling culture"; to Hoadley: "In both stock
  prediction and credit rating, the priority is accuracy. Interpretability is a secondary goal that
  can be finessed."; "Prove that you've got a better mousetrap and I'll buy it."
- Donoho 2017: data scientists should use both; the tension "is suppressed" in GDS.
- The disagreement is TAUGHT, NOT RESOLVED: the decision guide on S6 is framed as the questions
  each discussant would ask, never as a verdict.
- Tukey 1962 (read at source, pp.13-14) "Far better an approximate answer to the right question ..."
  and Cox's 2001 "Better a rough answer ..., due perhaps to Lord Kelvin": the same idea, two
  attributions; teach both.
